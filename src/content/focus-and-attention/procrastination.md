It is Monday night, and you have an exam on Friday covering 6 chapters you haven't opened. You meant to start tonight. Instead you have been scrolling for an hour, and now you are lying in bed, wide awake and anxious about it. If you care this much, why can't you just start?

This page covers what procrastination is and how it differs from laziness, two feelings that commonly keep you from starting, the easy escape that makes giving in so tempting, a move for each, and what to do after you've slipped anyway.

## It isn't laziness

A lazy person doesn't want to study and doesn't mind not studying. That isn't you. You want to study, you planned to, and putting it off makes you feel worse. Psychologist Piers Steel defines procrastination along those lines: voluntarily delaying something you intend to do, even though you expect the delay to leave you worse off.

So what is the hour of scrolling doing for you? Open the textbook and notice what you feel: dread about Friday, confusion about chapter 3, boredom. The phone makes those feelings go away within seconds. Psychologists Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl argue that this is the heart of procrastination. You are avoiding the bad feeling the task brings up, and you trade your future self's time for feeling better now.

Managing how you feel is called **emotion regulation**, and on this view procrastination is a short-term version of it that backfires. That is why a better calendar won't fix it on its own. You already know the exam is Friday; the trouble is the feeling you get when you sit down.

So there are two levers: make the feeling smaller, or take away the escape. Start with the feelings.

## Fear of failure

You hesitate to open chapter 1 because a bad practice quiz would prove you are behind. Not starting keeps that verdict away for one more night.

Compare the two risks honestly. If you study and do poorly at first, you find out which chapters are weak while there are still Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday to fix them. If you wait until Thursday night, you find the same weak chapters with no time left to fix them. Doing nothing feels safe tonight, but it is the riskier choice. Fear inaction more than failure: a bad first attempt is information, and no attempt gives you none.

That handles the fear of doing badly. A different feeling shows up when the job just looks too big.

## Feeling overwhelmed

"Study 6 chapters" is not something you can do in one sitting, so it feels like a wall instead of a step. Looking at the whole job at once is what freezes you.

Shrink the job you face right now:

1. Pick the single most important task. Chapters build on each other, so here that's chapter 1.
2. Commit to working on it for 10 minutes, and nothing more.
3. When the 10 minutes are up, you may stop. You may also keep going.

That's the **10-minute rule**, a practical trick rather than a lab-tested finding. What you are dreading shrinks from "6 chapters" to "10 minutes," a small enough feeling to face. Once you're inside the chapter, vague dread turns into concrete questions, which are easier to keep working on, so you'll often keep going. On the nights you stop, you still have 10 minutes you didn't have before.

Starting is now cheap. What's left is the phone next to the book, offering a better time than chapter 1 ever will.

## Take away the escape

Next to a feed that might show you something new with every swipe, a textbook feels slow. [Focus and Distraction](/focus-and-attention/why-you-cant-focus-anymore) explains why that pull is so strong. Here the phone is the quickest way out of the bad feeling, so the main move is to put it out of reach, with a short pause as a smaller second one.

- **Remove the distraction before you start.** Treat study time like a sports practice, where you wouldn't watch videos mid-drill. Focus and Distraction lists the ways: notifications off, phone in another room, logged out of the app. When chapter 3 gets confusing, the escape is no longer one tap away.
- **Pause before you begin.** If you come straight from scrolling, your mind is still buzzing. Sit for 2 minutes and breathe slowly, with nothing to look at, then open the book. You'll see this pitched online as resetting your [dopamine](/focus-and-attention/why-you-cant-focus-anymore) (a brain chemical tied to reward) "baseline," a claim no study supports. It can still help: it gives the urge to check the phone a moment to pass, and it marks the start of the session.

You'll still slip some nights, and how you respond decides whether the next night goes better.

## After you slip

Say Monday was lost to scrolling. The tempting response is to call yourself hopeless, but that adds a fresh bad feeling to the task, which is exactly what made you avoid it.

A study by Michael Wohl and colleagues, Pychyl among them, followed first-year students across two rounds of exams. Students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before the first exam procrastinated less before the second. Nobody was assigned to forgive themselves, so this shows a link, not proof, but it fits the picture: less guilt means less to avoid. Forgiving yourself means admitting you put it off, then starting Tuesday's 10 minutes without dragging Monday along.

**Rule of thumb.** When you can't make yourself start, shrink the feeling or remove the escape: remember that not starting is the bigger risk, make the first step 10 minutes, put the phone out of reach, and forgive yesterday's delay instead of adding guilt to today's task.
