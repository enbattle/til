summary: Why a dense page gets harder to stay with when your phone is nearby, and which fixes have research behind them.
date: 2026-09-14

Say you sit down to read a dense 30-minute chapter, with your phone face-down next to the book. Within a few minutes you have read the same paragraph twice, picked up the phone, put it down, and started over. You were not lazy, and the chapter did not get harder. Why does a phone you are not even using make a hard page so much harder to stay with?

This page covers what pulls you toward the phone, what each glance costs when you come back, and three changes to your environment that follow from both.

## Why does the phone pull so hard?

Take a vending machine that always gives you a snack. You can walk away from it easily. Now take one that gives a snack only occasionally, at random. That one is hard to leave, because the next try might pay off. A reward that arrives after an unpredictable number of tries is called a **variable reward**, and slot machines are built on it. Behavioral psychology has long found that animals and people tend to keep responding under this schedule, often more persistently than when the reward comes on a fixed pattern.

Your phone works like the second machine. Every pull-to-refresh might show something interesting, or nothing. So while you read, part of you is asking whether the phone is about to be more interesting than the page.

That question is where **dopamine** comes in. It is a chemical messenger in the brain, often called "the pleasure chemical," but a better-supported picture treats it as part of a **prediction signal**. It tracks whether something better than expected might be about to happen, and it helps decide what is worth pursuing next. A feed may send that signal far more often than a dense page does.

It is a hypothesis, not a settled finding, that a day of frequent checking makes a dense page feel slow by comparison. Better supported is the pull itself, and that the pull makes the next check likely. The pull gets you to the phone. The next question is what the glance costs.

## What does a check cost you?

Suppose you give in and glance at one notification, then return to the chapter. Is the glance free?

Researcher Sophie Leroy gave the cost a name: **attention residue**. In her experiments, people who switched away from an unfinished task kept part of their attention on it, and they did worse on the next task than people who had been able to finish the first. Leroy proposed that a lack of closure on the first task is what leaves the residue.

Now put it on the chapter. Suppose you check the phone 6 times in the 30 minutes. That is 6 times you come back with part of your mind still on whatever the feed showed you, so you reread the paragraph you were on. This number is made up for illustration, but the structure is not: the cost is measurable in lab studies, and what matters is how many switches add up.

Don't picture a single glance wiping out your reading. Picture the two mechanisms feeding each other. The feed pulls you out, and the residue makes the way back slower, which makes the page feel even duller next to the phone.

So the problem has two parts, and each needs its own fix.

## What helps, and what the research supports

The "dopamine detox" trend traces to psychologist Cameron Sepah's "dopamine fasting," a cognitive-behavioral technique for compulsive habits that he said was not meant literally. Its useful core is **stimulus control**: change the environment so the trigger is not there, instead of resisting it with willpower. For the chapter, that gives three changes.

- **Remove the trigger.** Turn off notifications that are not urgent, and put the phone in another room. Log out of an app instead of trusting yourself not to open it. This removes the cue, so there are fewer of the 6 checks to begin with. A study by Adrian Ward and colleagues found that a phone on the desk lowered scores on a working-memory test (how much you can hold in mind at once) compared with leaving it in another room. Direct replications have mostly found no effect, and a later review found only a small one, so treat the other room as a sensible default, not a proven effect. It costs almost nothing, and that is the better reason to do it.
- **Leave a stopping note.** If you must stop partway through, write one line on where you are and what comes next. Research on unfinished goals, by Masicampo and Baumeister, found that making a concrete plan for the goal reduced its intrusion into later tasks. Leroy and Glomb later found a similar benefit for writing a plan to resume. That gives your attention something to put down.
- **Protect a block.** Decide in advance on a stretch, say 30 to 60 minutes, during which you will not switch. Nothing is special about those numbers. Fewer switches mean less residue to clear.

None of this assumes your brain is broken. It is responding in a normal way to an environment that is unusually good at producing unpredictable rewards, so the fix is mostly to change the environment and how you hand off between tasks, rather than to push harder against it.

**Rule of thumb.** If you want to hold your attention on something hard, take away the thing that interrupts you, and leave a note before you stop, so you don't need willpower to stay with the task or to return to it.
